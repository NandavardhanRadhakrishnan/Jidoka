# Office playbooks validate Jidoka but demand clocks

Across finance, customer service, sales ops, IT, HR, legal and procurement, office work reduces to a few dozen recurring work items per function. Each runs on the same playbook shape: classify the item, gather context from a system of record, follow a deterministic decision tree with a few judgment gates, and send the exceptions to a person. That is Jidoka's model almost exactly. On classification, Jidoka is ahead of most incumbents: it asks the user when triage is ambiguous, saves the answer as an example, and re-triages open tasks when a type changes, and no surveyed product does all three. The analyst evidence also favours its "compile the rule once, keep AI steps bounded" design over free-roaming agents. The gaps sit somewhere else. Real playbooks run on **time and other people**: work pauses while someone else replies, dunning and SLA ladders escalate at day 31, 61 and 91, approvals sit in the middle of a procedure, and some content (bank-detail changes, harassment, chargebacks) must never be auto-actioned whatever the rule says. Jidoka's model is event in, state out, and it has no first-class notion of waiting, timers, mid-rule approval or hard guardrails. Fixing those four matters more than adding further step types. On fit, Jidoka suits individual knowledge workers, small professional-services firms and the exception residue of larger finance and service teams better than enterprise functions that already route work through dedicated portals. The evidence behind the patterns is good; the evidence behind volumes and percentages is mostly vendor-sourced and, in places, a decade old.

## Office work reduces to a few dozen recurring work items per function

Every serious framework analyses automation at the level of the task, not the job. McKinsey estimates generative AI reaches activities that absorb **60-70% of employee time**, largely because natural-language understanding is needed for activities that make up about 25% of all work time ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-economic-potential-of-generative-ai-the-next-productivity-frontier)). Stanford's WORKBank rated **844 tasks across 104 occupations** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06576)). Anthropic's telemetry shows Office & Administrative Support rising to **13% of Claude.ai transcripts**, mostly email management, document processing, CRM work and scheduling ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)). Inbound volume is the forcing function. Microsoft telemetry puts the average worker at **117 emails and 153 Teams messages a day**, with an interruption every two minutes ([Microsoft WorkLab](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday)). Jidoka's unit of work, one inbound item typed and routed individually, is the same unit the research uses.

Process frameworks give the top level of the taxonomy. APQC's Process Classification Framework splits finance into revenue accounting (credit, billing, AR, collections, deductions), general accounting and close, AP and expense reimbursement, treasury, internal controls and tax ([APQC](https://www.apqc.org/sites/default/files/K04087_8.0_Manage_Financial_Resources.pdf)). ITIL splits IT work into incidents, service requests and standard, normal and emergency changes ([Faddom](https://faddom.com/itil-change-management-types-standard-vs-normal-vs-emergency/)). But nothing arrives in a queue as "Manage treasury operations." Real items sit one or two levels below the framework: "has my invoice been paid," "please update our bank details," "short payment on invoice 4471." AppZen's April 2026 AP product names six such items as the core of an AP inbox: payment status inquiries, bank change requests, duplicate invoices, vendor statement reconciliation, W-9 submissions and remittance requests ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/appzen-launches-ap-inbox-service-center-bringing-ai-agents-to-the-most-manual-process-in-accounts-payable-302738537.html)). Jidoka's type registry should be built at that level.

The catalog shows three structural regularities. First, **volume concentrates in the head of the distribution.** Practitioners recommend 30-50 support categories, yet teams drift to 400-500, at which point agents pick the first "good enough" tag ([SentiSum](https://www.sentisum.com/customer-service-analytics/help-desk-ticket-categories-best-practices)). Roughly 20 of 100 categories carry 80% of volume ([MetricNet](https://www.metricnet.com/contact-center-metrics-essentials-module-2-the-80-20-rule/)), and a single reason such as WISMO can reach 20-40% of e-commerce tickets ([ClickPost](https://www.clickpost.ai/en-us/blog/what-is-a-wismo-complaint)). Jidoka users should therefore expect tens of types, and onboarding the top five to ten covers most volume. Second, **every inbox mixes three classes** that need different handling: documents to process, inquiries that need a lookup and a reply, and high-risk change requests. The third class is adversarial. The AFP 2025 survey found **79% of organisations hit by attempted or actual payments fraud**, business email compromise as the top avenue at **63%**, and vendor-imposter fraud up from 34% to **45%** ([AFP via Truist](https://www.truist.com/content/dam/truist-bank/us/en/documents/info/cci/2025-afp-payments-fraud-control-survey-report-key-highlights.pdf)). Third, **the human residue is the exceptions.** The average AP shop processes only **35.4% of invoices straight-through** with an **18.4% exception rate**, and only **4% of AP teams have fully automated exception management** ([Ardent Partners 2025](https://d15fjz85703yz4.cloudfront.net/1517/5157/1685/Ardent_Partners_-_State_of_ePayables_2025_-_Bottomline_-_FINAL.pdf)). ERPs and AP suites already handle the straight-through work. What is left over is unstructured, email-borne and context-hungry, which is the niche where Jidoka's classify, gather context and hand off pattern fits.

## Company size changes the pipes and the controls, not the task types

The same task types recur from a global business-services centre down to a five-person firm. What changes is the channel the work arrives on, who owns the procedure, and how many controls surround it.

| Dimension | MNC / shared services | Mid-market | Small and micro business |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where work arrives | Function-specific portals, ERP exception queues, ITSM and HRSD tools. Email persists: it is the highest-volume vendor-inquiry channel for **85%** of shared-services respondents ([Peeriosity](https://www.peeriosity.com/shared-services/articles/2022/03/eliminating-vendor-phone-inquiries-in-accounts-payable/)) | Helpdesk or shared inbox. For Jidoka, the important point is that **cross-function triage from a single stream is a small-firm and individual-worker problem**. In an MNC, the AP clerk, the IT agent and the HR generalist each already receive pre-sorted work through their own portal. Jidoka's value there is the residue: the shared mailbox nobody owns, or one knowledge worker's inbox. A small firm's single inbox mixes an IT issue, a vendor invoice, a leave request and a prospect's NDA. That is exactly the mixed stream an evolving type registry is built for.

Two small-business findings cut in opposite directions. Owners rarely have written SOPs but can usually say how they handle something. Jidoka's natural-language onboarding therefore converts tacit knowledge into an executable, documented procedure as a by-product, and the people who never write SOPs are the ones who most need that. The same segment names setup as its biggest barrier. **36.8%** of Indian MSMEs find setting up digital tools challenging, against single digits for finding customers or finance ([India SME Forum](https://indiasmeforum.org/digishaastra/assets/docs/Final-META-Report-Card-2025.pdf)). **44%** of US owners lack the resources or expertise to deploy AI ([Fox Business on Goldman Sachs](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/small-business-ai-adoption-jumps-68-owners-plan-significant-workforce-growth-2025)). Half of G7 non-adopting SMEs cite missing staff skills ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/11/generative-ai-and-the-sme-workforce_83bafdfb/2d08b99d-en.pdf)). Bring-your-own API keys, MCP server configuration and rule review are exactly the steps this segment stalls on. The same OECD survey also shows the opposite pull: **52%** of non-adopters worry about what happens to data fed into models. That makes Jidoka's local-first, BYO-provider design a real selling point to the same people who find it hard to set up.

Adoption depth is the white space. Association and vendor surveys report 58-76% of US small businesses "using AI" ([US Chamber](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/empowering-small-business-the-impact-of-technology-on-u-s-small-business); [Intuit](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/r/small-business-data/april-2025-survey/)). The Census Bureau's BTOS finds only **8.8%** using AI to produce goods or services ([SBA Advocacy](https://advocacy.sba.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Research-Spotlight-AI-in-Business-Small-Firms-Closing-In_-092425.pdf)), and only **13-14%** call AI core or fully embedded. Enterprises look similar. McKinsey finds **23%** of firms scaling agents anywhere and no more than **10%** in any single function ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai)). The gap between chatting with AI and having AI wired into a workflow is large at every company size, and that gap is the space Jidoka occupies.

## Jidoka's triage-plus-compiled-rule model matches where the evidence points

**Type representation is on the industry consensus.** The market has moved from trained classifiers to "short name plus natural-language description, classified by an LLM." Salesforce Agentforce routes on each subagent's "classification description" ([Salesforce](https://developer.salesforce.com/blogs/2025/01/how-to-write-effective-natural-language-instructions-for-agentforce)). Zendesk custom intents carry a 650-character description "explaining what the topic includes/excludes" ([Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/8718789695002-Personalizing-intelligent-triage-by-creating-custom-intents)). Jidoka's name, description and examples is the same representation.

**Ambiguity handling and taxonomy maintenance are where Jidoka leads.** Almost every incumbent handles ambiguity with a confidence threshold and a fallback queue. Zendesk's default is 60 on a 0-100 scale, and practitioners use 70-80 ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/zendesk-ai-agent-intent-confidence-threshold), competitor). No surveyed product asks the user "is this A or B?" and saves the answer as a training example. Help Scout states outright that "manual changes do not currently feed back into the AI model," and it keeps old classifications when topics change ([Help Scout](https://docs.helpscout.com/article/1773-categorize-conversations-automatically-with-ai-topics)). No product was found that re-triages open items after a taxonomy edit. These are real differentiators. Their cost is user attention: no source quantifies what share of real inbound items are type-ambiguous, so the ask rate is an open risk to measure.

**Compile-once rules with bounded AI steps are what analysts recommend.** Gartner predicts **over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027**. It advises using agents for decisions, traditional automation for routine workflows and assistants for retrieval ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027)). Anthropic's guidance prefers predefined workflow paths over autonomous agents unless the flexibility is needed ([Anthropic](https://resources.anthropic.com/hubfs/Building%20Effective%20AI%20Agents-%20Architecture%20Patterns%20and%20Implementation%20Frameworks.pdf)). Forrester's new Adaptive Process Orchestration category is defined as deterministic plus nondeterministic control flow, with governance ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/announcing-the-evaluation-of-the-adaptive-process-orchestration-market/)). Benchmarks agree. On Salesforce's CRMArena-Pro, agents reach about **58% success single-turn and 35% multi-turn**, but **over 83% on workflow execution** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18878)). The best agent on TheAgentCompany completes only **30.3%** of realistic office tasks, and it does worst on tasks that involve chatting with colleagues ([NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/0d744742f6fac4d1134c019b7cef3c8a-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)). Real SOPs have the same shape, as the catalog shows. ITIL standard changes, dunning ladders, refund thresholds and clause playbooks are all deterministic trees with a few judgment gates. That is a rule with `branch` steps fed by `ai` outputs.

**Pre-processing for a human is the best-measured win in the field.** Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's study of **5,172 support agents** found an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by about **15%**, with the largest gains for novices ([QJE](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658)). The BCG/HBS field experiment found 25% faster work and over 40% higher quality on tasks inside the AI's competence. On a task outside it, AI users were **19 percentage points less likely** to be correct ([Dell'Acqua et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374015542_Navigating_the_Jagged_Technological_Frontier_Field_Experimental_Evidence_of_the_Effects_of_AI_on_Knowledge_Worker_Productivity_and_Quality)). That jagged frontier is an argument *for* per-type routing: the same model helps on some types and hurts on others, and a per-type rule is where that decision gets encoded. Worker preference points the same way. WORKBank finds "equal partnership" (H3) is the dominant desired level in **45.2% of occupations** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06576)). Jidoka's "summarise, gather context, assign to human" path sits right there.

**Smaller design choices also line up.** Approving write tools once at rule activation mirrors ITIL's pre-authorisation of a standard change's *procedure* rather than each execution. Using MCP instead of UI automation avoids the brittleness that sank RPA. Deloitte found process fragmentation was the number-one RPA barrier year after year, and only 3-13% of firms ever scaled past 50 bots ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/intelligent-automation-2022-survey-results.html)). On procedure authoring, Jidoka's onboarding (describe the handling, an agent builds a declarative rule, the user reviews) is the same pattern as Intercom's "paste your SOP and Fin structures it," Decagon's AOP Copilot and Sierra's Ghostwriter ([fin.ai](https://fin.ai/learn/ai-agent-procedures-aops-journeys); [Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/agent-studio-2-0)). Jidoka also has no direct equivalent in the market. Enterprise CX platforms are customer-facing and priced per seat plus per outcome, for example Fin at $0.99 per outcome ([ClearFeed](https://clearfeed.ai/blogs/intercom-pricing)). SMB agent builders are cloud and credit-priced, and Lindy's complaint record of credits burned by build errors and emails sent to the wrong recipients is a warning about exactly the trust points Jidoka must get right ([getmacha](https://www.getmacha.com/blog/lindy-ai-complete-guide), competitor).

The strongest counter-argument concerns representation. Fin Procedures and Decagon AOPs keep the playbook as natural language that the LLM interprets at runtime, with code conditions embedded where needed. Jidoka compiles natural language into explicit steps. That makes it more inspectable and auditable, which suits the governance gap: Deloitte finds only **21%** of firms have mature agent governance ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/docs/about/2025/state-of-ai-2026-global.pdf)). It is also more brittle against intra-type variance. Celonis found Siemens' order-to-cash process contained about **923,000 variants** ([Celonis](https://www.celonis.com/solutions/stories/siemens-digital-transformation-process-mining), vendor). Jidoka's answer is `ai` steps at branch points plus learned per-step hints. That answer is reasonable but unproven at volume.

A note on current state: the codebase already goes beyond the model described for this review. It extracts deadlines at triage, asks for confirmation on cross-source duplicates, reopens a done task when its source reports a new revision (resuming the agent session), stores per-step learned hints, offers "draft" handoff targets and has a `failed` state. A relevance-check (`not_relevant`) outcome is designed but not yet built. The gap analysis below accounts for these.

## Real playbooks run on clocks, approvals and other people

Across every domain, real playbooks need things beyond "classify, run steps, assign." The table ranks the gaps by how often the evidence demands them and how much they would change Jidoka's fit for its best-matched users.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Waiting states with auto-resume** | HRSD pause states with maximum durations: awaiting employee (5 business days), awaiting vendor (10), awaiting manager or legal (3), awaiting effective date. The top four belong together. Office work is a conversation that unfolds over days with other parties, and Jidoka currently models it as a single pass from item to state. The HR, AP and small-business evidence all describe the same loop: act, wait for someone, resume on their reply or on a deadline, escalate if neither comes. Jidoka's reopen-on-revision mechanism is the right primitive for the "resume on reply" half. It still lacks a visible waiting state that names who is being waited on, a timeout that fires without any inbound event, and sources that emit tasks from data conditions rather than messages. Payment chasing is the clearest single test case. It is the best-measured small-business pain point, the playbook is easy to state in natural language, and the trigger lives in the ledger rather than the inbox. Jidoka's architecture can already express it as a ledger-polling extension plus a dunning rule with a draft-and-approve send. Only the scheduled re-evaluation is missing.

Approve-then-act matters because it is where trust is won or lost. Jidoka's activation-time gate answers "may this rule ever call this write tool?" It does not answer "may it send *this* email to *this* vendor now?" For external communication and anything that moves money, every surveyed product and every trust survey points to per-execution approval as the default. Teams then loosen it per type or per action once accuracy is proven. Guardrails are needed for a related reason. Jidoka's triage and agent steps read attacker-controllable content, and BEC is the top payments-fraud vector. A type flagged "never auto-act; human verifies out of band" protects users even when a generated rule is wrong.

The learning loop is no longer a gap. HFS argues deployments stall at "assisted execution" without a continuous learning loop ([HFS](https://www.hfsresearch.com/research/agentic-ai-learning-automation/)), and MIT NANDA ties success to systems that adapt ([Virtualization Review](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx)). Jidoka's saved triage examples, learned per-step hints and the agentic-regeneration design already form that loop. What it still lacks is outcome signal: whether a human overrode, reassigned or reopened the task. Feeding that signal back would show which rules need revision.

## The evidence is strong on patterns and weak on volumes

Confidence in this report varies sharply by claim type. The **structural patterns** are well supported because independent kinds of source converge on them. These are the playbook shapes, the human-held judgment gates, exceptions as the residue, and per-procedure pre-authorisation. The sources include process frameworks (APQC, ITIL), primary product documentation (Zendesk, Intercom, Help Scout, Linear, Salesforce), association surveys (AFP, CLOC, NAPEO), government data (the UK Small Business Commissioner, US Census BTOS, OECD) and peer-reviewed field studies. The **analyst outlook** is mostly survey or prediction. Gartner's 40% cancellation and 80%-of-common-issues-by-2029 figures are forecasts. Adoption numbers conflict because definitions differ: KPMG reports 42% "deployed," PwC 79% "adopting," McKinsey and Deloitte 23% scaling or moderate use. The MIT NANDA "95% of pilots fail" figure has drawn wide methodological criticism ([agentmodeai](https://agentmodeai.com/the-mit-genai-pilot-failure-claim/)).

The **volumes and percentages** are the weakest layer. Several are vendor numbers: tag accuracy (60-70% manual vs 89-96% AI), the claim that 30% of tickets need reassignment, NDA self-service rates, order-entry time shares, RPA maintenance costs, and almost every small-business admin-hours split. Others are old: the 30% password-reset figure is from 2011, the GBTA expense benchmark from 2015, the lead-response studies from 2007 and 2011, and EY's "30-50% of RPA projects fail" from 2016. Several important quantities could not be found at all. No neutral source breaks down AP or AR inbox composition by item type. None measures the share of inbound items that are type-ambiguous, which drives Jidoka's ask rate, or the share of tickets that enter a waiting state. There is no rigorous data on SOP prevalence in small firms. Facilities and office administration, customer success, and tax and compliance admin were not researched in usable depth, so the catalog rows for those areas are thin or absent. Several Gartner, McKinsey and OECD figures were reached only through secondary summaries because the primary pages were paywalled or returned errors. Treat any single percentage here as directional and the cross-source patterns as reliable.

## Conclusion

The research reframes Jidoka's main open question. The registry and compile-once rules are not where Jidoka is behind. On classification it already beats products with far larger teams, and its rule model is the profile analysts expect to survive the agent shake-out. It is behind on the time dimension of work. An office playbook is a small state machine that spans days and involves people outside the tool: a requester who has to send a receipt, a vendor who has to confirm, a ledger date that ticks past 30 days, an approver who has to click yes. A next roadmap built around a waiting state with owners and timeouts, scheduled re-evaluation, data-condition sources and per-execution approve-then-act would turn Jidoka from an inbox sorter with playbooks into something that can carry an AP exception, a dunning ladder or an HR case from start to finish.

The evidence also implies a positioning choice. Jidoka's single-stream, cross-function triage is the right shape for individual knowledge workers, small professional-services firms and bookkeepers, and the unowned shared mailbox inside larger firms. It is a poor fit for enterprise functions that already route work through dedicated portals. For those first users, the deciding factors will be setup cost and trust rather than step expressiveness: starter types and rules for the common head items (payment chase, quote request, status inquiry, meeting request, invoice to file), bulk discovery from existing mail, a replay test before a rule goes live, and a default that drafts rather than sends.
